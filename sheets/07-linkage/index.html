Method

Every joint is found as the intersection of two circles. For the four bar, A sits on the crank circle around O2, and B is where a circle of radius "coupler" around A meets a circle of radius "rocker" around O4. There are two answers, the open and the crossed assembly.

If the input link can turn all the way around, it is a crank: the sheet keeps one assembly for the whole turn. If it can't, it is a rocker, and the angles that assemble split into separate intervals, one per circuit (the real linkage can't get from one circuit to the other without being taken apart). The input then swings between the two limits of its circuit. At a limit the coupler and rocker line up, the two assemblies meet, and the motion carries on through that position on the other assembly while the input reverses. One full swing, out and back, traces the circuit's whole closed coupler curve. "Switch circuit" moves to the other interval; for a crank it swaps open and crossed. For a rocker input the slider runs through one swing in percent instead of an angle. The swing is paced so the input slows near the limits.

d = |O4 − A| a = (r1² − r2² + d²) / (2d) h = √(r1² − a²) B = A + a (O4 − A)/d ± h · perpendicular Grashof: s + l ≤ p + q (shortest + longest vs the other two)

Grashof class: if s + l < p + q, at least one link can turn all the way around, and which one is shortest decides the type (shortest is the ground: double crank; the crank: crank rocker; the coupler: double rocker; the rocker: rocker crank). If s + l = p + q it is a change point linkage, which can flip between branches at a singular position. Otherwise it is a non Grashof triple rocker. The transmission angle μ is the angle between coupler and rocker at B, folded into 0° to 90°; values far from 90° mean the linkage pushes badly.

Toggle positions: the sheet lists crank angles where crank and coupler line up (the rocker is at the end of its swing) and, for a crank that can't turn fully, where coupler and rocker line up (the crank stops). Coupler point velocity is a central finite difference of position with a 1e-6 rad step, times the crank speed.

Jansen leg: the crank center O is at (0, 0) and the frame pivot B is at (−a, −l). With crank tip A, the joints are found in order: C from (B, b) and (A, j); D from (B, c) and (A, k); E from (B, d) and (C, e); F from (D, g) and (E, f); the foot G from (D, i) and (F, h). Triangles BCE and DFG are rigid. Units are whatever the holy numbers are in (Jansen's drawings use cm). Ground contact is defined as the part of the foot path within 2 percent of the step height above its lowest point; stride is the horizontal length of that part.

Checks: loop closure (every bar length matches its input) is tested at every solved angle, and the Jansen joints at 0° are cross checked against a separate Newton solve of all ten bar equations.

References: Norton, Design of Machinery; Theo Jansen's holy numbers (strandbeest.com); the Jansen joint construction on Wikibooks. Full list in docs/references.md. Benchmark checks: Verification sheet.
